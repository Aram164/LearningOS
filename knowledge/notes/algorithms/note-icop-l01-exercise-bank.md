# ICOP 01: Linear Programming - Exercise Bank

These ten original tasks target the complete chapter. Source tags name the supporting **physical PDF pages** in Kratsch's `ico_linearprogramming.pdf`, WS 2026/27. Questions and solutions are separated so each task can first be attempted without a model answer. The running LP is deliberately reused across modeling, forms, geometry, simplex, and certificates.

## Questions

### E1. Model and transform a production problem

Two divisible products have returns $3$ and $2$ per unit. Resource A allows $4$ units and usages are $1$ for each product. Resource B allows $6$ units and usages are $2$ and $1$. Formulate an LP using quantities $u,v$. State what changes if only whole units may be made. Give both the course canonical and course standard forms, the full matrix $A$, vectors $b,c$, and the recovery rule for the objective value. Explain why merely introducing slacks without sign restrictions is incorrect. [Deck pp. 6-10, 14, 19, 21-22.]

### E2. Free and nonpositive variables

Transform

$$\min 2x-3t\quad\text{s.t. }x+t=1,\quad x-2t\ge-2,\quad t\le0,\quad x\text{ free}$$

to the course standard form with only nonnegative variables. Then give the canonical-form inequalities before adding slacks. Specify the map back to $(x,t)$ and explain why splitting a free variable need not give a one-to-one representation. [Deck pp. 15-21.]

### E3. Geometry, optimal faces, and boundedness

For the feasible set from E1, list all vertices and identify each by its tight rows, including sign constraints. Evaluate the E1 objective at each vertex. Replace the objective by $u+v$ and describe **every** optimizer. Prove that a local minimum of a linear function on a convex set is global. Give an example where the feasible set is unbounded but the minimization objective has a finite optimum. [Deck pp. 25-40.]

### E4. Basic feasible solutions and the vertex theorem

Consider $P=\{(x_1,x_2)\ge0:x_1+x_2=1\}$. Classify $(1,0)$, $(1/2,1/2)$, and $(0,1)$ using support-column independence and the extreme-point definition. Prove the direction “dependent support columns imply not extreme” for a general $Ax=b,x\ge0$. Explain how to move from an optimal non-vertex toward a smaller-support optimal solution. Does a feasible LP with arbitrary free variables always have a vertex? [Deck pp. 44-55.]

### E5. Carry out the simplex pivots

Use E1's standard-form minimization, with variable order $(u,v,s_1,s_2)$. Start at the slack basis. Choose $u$ first and then any improving variable. At each step provide the basis, basic values, entering variable's reduced cost, direction $A_B^{-1}a_j$, ratio test, leaving variable, and objective. Stop with an algebraic optimality certificate using all final reduced costs. Explain what would certify unboundedness instead, and identify where nondegeneracy matters. [Deck pp. 67-81.]

### E6. Phase I distinguishes feasibility from infeasibility

For $x_1-x_2=1$, $x_1+x_2=3$, $x_1,x_2\ge0$, write the phase-I LP and find a sequence of feasible pivots from its artificial identity basis to zero artificial objective. For $x_1+x_2=-1$, $x_1,x_2\ge0$, normalize the right side and determine the phase-I optimum. Prove why an auxiliary optimum of zero is equivalent to original feasibility and why phase I cannot report unboundedness below. [Deck pp. 82-87.]

### E7. Derive a mixed-sign dual rather than memorizing names

Use $r=-t$ to rewrite E2 with $r\ge0$ and $x$ free. Derive its dual using one variable per row. Explain why one dual variable is free and why the constraint for $x$ is an equality. Find a feasible primal/dual pair with equal objective values. For a standard-form minimization LP, prove weak duality and show how $y^T=c_B^TA_B^{-1}$ links a basis with nonnegative reduced costs to a dual certificate. [Deck pp. 94-112.]

### E8. Complementary slackness and a false converse

Derive the upper-bound dual of E1's original maximization LP. Use complementary slackness, assuming $u,v>0$, to recover its dual optimum and then certify the primal optimum. Now change the return to $2u+2v$ and find an optimal primal/dual pair with a tight primal resource row whose dual multiplier is zero. State and prove the gap decomposition for a feasible max-$\le$ pair, and explain why checking products without checking feasibility is insufficient. [Deck pp. 111, 117-123.]

### E9. Outcomes and infeasibility certificates

Derive the dual and classify both sides for each pair generated by these standard-form primals:

1. $\min x$ subject to $x-s=1$, $x,s\ge0$.
2. $\min -x$ subject to $x-s=0$, $x,s\ge0$.
3. $\min -x$ subject to $0x=1$, $x\ge0$.

Which implications follow from weak duality, and which need strong duality? Separately, for $Az\le b$ with $A=(1,-1)^T$ and $b=(0,-1)^T$, find $y\ge0$ with $y^TA=0$, $y^Tb<0$, and use it to refute feasibility. Does this argument alone prove that every infeasible inequality system has such a certificate? [Deck pp. 96-102, 113-114, 128.]

### E10. Transfer proofs and complexity

(a) A warehouse is the triangle $z_1\ge0,z_2\ge0,z_1+z_2\le6$. Formulate an LP for the center and radius of its largest contained Euclidean ball, and solve it. Explain why a Euclidean norm appears without making the LP nonlinear.

(b) Prove that a vertex of $Q=\{x:Ax\le b,0\le x\le U\}$ has at most $m$ coordinates strictly between their bounds when $A$ has $m$ rows. Deduce the bound of $m$ noninteger coordinates for an optimal vertex when $U$ is integer and the LP is feasible.

(c) Explain why polynomial work per simplex pivot and finite termination do not imply polynomial total running time. Contrast vertex enumeration and simplex; identify what the chapter develops versus merely lists as further algorithmic approaches. [Deck pp. 59-60, 88-91, 126, 128.]

## Solutions and proof checks

### E1 solution

The model is $\max 3u+2v$ subject to $u+v\le4$, $2u+v\le6$, $u,v\ge0$. Whole-unit production adds $u,v\in\mathbb Z$, producing an ILP. The course canonical form is $\min -3u-2v$ with those inequalities. Its standard form adds $s_1,s_2\ge0$ and equalities $u+v+s_1=4$, $2u+v+s_2=6$:

$$A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&0\\2&1&0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad b=\binom46,\quad c=\begin{pmatrix}-3\\-2\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}.$$

Read $u,v$ directly from a transformed solution and negate the minimum objective to recover maximum return. A free slack could become negative and accommodate a resource overuse, so its nonnegativity is essential. These form names are the deck's Blum convention, not universal names.

### E2 solution

Set $x=p-q$, $t=-r$ with $p,q,r\ge0$. The objective becomes $2p-2q+3r$. Canonical inequalities are

$$p-q-r\le1,\quad-p+q+r\le-1,\quad-p+q-2r\le2.$$

A direct standard form is

$$p-q-r=1,\quad p-q+2r-s=-2,\quad p,q,r,s\ge0.$$

The second equality uses a nonnegative surplus $s$. Recover $x=p-q,t=-r$. Adding the same nonnegative number to $p$ and $q$ gives the same $x$ and the same original objective, so splitting is many-to-one. Negative right sides are allowed in standard form; phase I later flips such rows.

### E3 solution

The vertices and tight restrictions are $(0,0)$ ($u=0,v=0$), $(3,0)$ ($v=0,2u+v=6$), $(2,2)$ (both resource rows), and $(0,4)$ ($u=0,u+v=4$). Their returns are $0,9,10,8$, so $(2,2)$ is optimal. For $u+v$, the maximum is $4$ and all solutions are $(u,4-u)$ for $0\le u\le2$.

For the proof, if $c^Ty<c^Tx$ for feasible $x,y$, then $z=(1-t)x+ty$ is feasible and $c^Tz<c^Tx$ for every $0<t\le1$. For arbitrarily small $t$, this contradicts local optimality at $x$. The LP $\min x$ subject to $x\ge0$ has an unbounded feasible ray but finite minimum $0$.

### E4 solution

The single-column supports of the endpoints are independent, so the endpoints are BFS. At $(1/2,1/2)$ both columns of $A=(1\ 1)$ are used and are dependent; $d=(1/2,-1/2)$ gives two distinct feasible points symmetrically around it.

Generally, dependence gives $Ad=0$ for some nonzero $d$ supported on the positive coordinates of $x$. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ smaller than every $x_j/|d_j|$ for nonzero $d_j$. Then $x\pm\varepsilon d\ge0$ and $A(x\pm\varepsilon d)=b$, refuting extremality. If $x$ is optimal, both directions being feasible force $c^Td=0$. Choose a sign with a negative component and move the largest feasible distance in that direction. At least one positive coordinate becomes zero, no existing zero becomes nonzero, and cost stays optimal. Repetition reaches independent support. The free-variable LP $\min 0x$ with $x\in\mathbb R$ has feasible set $\mathbb R$, all points optimal, and no extreme points.

### E5 solution - solved running LP

Initially $B=(s_1,s_2)$, $x_B=(4,6)$, objective $0$, and reduced costs for $(u,v)$ are $(-3,-2)$. Enter $u$. Direction $d=(1,2)$ gives ratios $4$ and $3$, so $\theta=3$ and $s_2$ leaves. New full vector: $(3,0,1,0)$, cost $-9$.

Now $B=(u,s_1)$, $x_B=(3,1)$, and

$$A_B^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1/2\\1&-1/2\end{pmatrix},\quad c_B^TA_B^{-1}=(0,-3/2).$$

The nonbasic variables are $(v,s_2)$, with reduced costs $(-1/2,3/2)$. Enter $v$. Its direction is $(1/2,1/2)$; ratios $6$ and $2$ give $\theta=2$ and $s_1$ leaves. The full vector becomes $(2,2,0,0)$, cost $-10$.

For $B=(u,v)$, $A_B^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}$, $x_B=(2,2)$, and $c_B^TA_B^{-1}=(-1,-1)$. Reduced costs for $(s_1,s_2)$ are $(1,1)$. Since every feasible nonbasic vector is nonnegative, its contribution to cost is nonnegative. Thus cost $-10$, or return $10$, is optimal.

An entering negative-reduced-cost variable with $d\le0$ would yield a feasible ray of strictly decreasing cost. Nondegeneracy ensures a positive step and, in this presentation, a unique leaving variable; without it a pivot may have zero step and the strict-improvement termination proof needs revision.

### E6 solution

Phase I minimizes $h_1+h_2$ subject to $x_1-x_2+h_1=1$, $x_1+x_2+h_2=3$, all variables nonnegative. Initially $(x_1,x_2,h_1,h_2)=(0,0,1,3)$. Entering $x_1$ gives direction $(1,1)$ and step $1$, producing $(1,0,0,2)$, with objective $2$. In basis $(x_1,h_2)$, increasing $x_2$ gives $x_1=1+x_2$, $h_2=2-2x_2$, and objective $2-2x_2$. Step $1$ gives $(2,1,0,0)$ and objective $0$.

The infeasible system is normalized to $-x_1-x_2=1$. Phase I has $-x_1-x_2+h=1$, hence $h=1+x_1+x_2\ge1$, with optimum $1$. If the original system is feasible, setting $h=0$ in phase I gives value zero. Conversely a zero value and $h\ge0$ force $h=0$, giving an original feasible $x$. The auxiliary objective is nonnegative, excluding unboundedness below.

### E7 solution

With $r=-t$, the primal is $\min 2x+3r$ subject to $x-r=1$, $x+2r\ge-2$, $r\ge0$, $x$ free. Let $a$ multiply the equality and $b$ multiply the inequality. The dual is

$$\max a-2b\quad\text{s.t. }a+b=2,\quad-a+2b\le3,\quad b\ge0,\quad a\text{ free}.$$

A free multiplier preserves an equality for either sign. A free primal $x$ can be arbitrarily positive or negative, so lower-bounding its coefficient requires exact equality. The primal point $(x,r)=(1,0)$ and dual point $(a,b)=(2,0)$ are feasible, both with value $2$, proving optimality.

For standard form, $b^Ty=y^TAx\le c^Tx$ whenever $A^Ty\le c$ and $x\ge0$. Put $y^T=c_B^TA_B^{-1}$. On basic columns $y^TA_B=c_B^T$; on nonbasic columns, $c_N^T-y^TA_N=\bar c_N^T\ge0$. Thus $y$ is dual feasible and $y^Tb=c_B^TA_B^{-1}b=c^Tx$ at the current BFS. Nonnegative reduced costs are an assumption to check, especially at a degenerate optimum.

### E8 solution

The dual is $\min4p+6q$ subject to $p+2q\ge3$, $p+q\ge2$, $p,q\ge0$. Positive $u,v$ force both dual inequalities tight: subtracting $p+q=2$ from $p+2q=3$ gives $q=1,p=1$. Positive $p,q$ force both primal resource rows tight, giving $u=v=2$. Both values are $10$.

For changed return $2u+2v$, use primal $(u,v)=(2,2)$ and dual $(p,q)=(2,0)$. Both values are $8$; the second primal row is tight but $q=0$. This refutes the converse “tight row implies positive multiplier.”

For max $c^Tx$ subject to $Ax\le b,x\ge0$ and min $b^Ty$ subject to $A^Ty\ge c,y\ge0$,

$$b^Ty-c^Tx=y^T(b-Ax)+(A^Ty-c)^Tx.$$

Expanding cancels $y^TAx$. Feasibility makes every product nonnegative, so zero gap is equivalent to all complementary products vanishing. Without feasibility the bound and nonnegativity can fail; zero products alone certify nothing about membership in either feasible set.

### E9 solution

1. The dual is $\max y$ subject to $y\le1$, $-y\le0$, with $y$ free before these column constraints. Thus $0\le y\le1$. Primal $(x,s)=(1,0)$ and dual $y=1$ have value $1$: both feasible and bounded.
2. The dual maximizes $0y$ subject to $y\le-1$, $-y\le0$, which is infeasible. The primal ray $x=s=t\ge0$ has cost $-t$, hence is unbounded below.
3. The primal equality $0x=1$ is infeasible. Its dual is $\max y$ subject to $0y\le-1$, also infeasible. This demonstrates that primal infeasibility does not force dual unboundedness.

Weak duality excludes a feasible LP opposite an unbounded dual and proves equal-value feasible certificates optimal. Strong duality supplies existence and equality of optimal dual solutions for a feasible primal with finite optimum. For the separate inequality system, $y=(1,1)$ gives $y^TA=0$ and $y^Tb=-1$. Any feasible $z$ would imply $0=y^TAz\le y^Tb=-1$, a contradiction. This proves sufficiency of a certificate, not its existence for every infeasible system.

### E10 solution

(a) The ball LP is

$$\max r\quad\text{s.t. }-z_1+r\le0,\ -z_2+r\le0,\ z_1+z_2+\sqrt2\,r\le6,\ r\ge0,$$

with free center coordinates. Each row says the maximum of $a_i^T(z+v)$ over $\|v\|_2\le r$ is at most $b_i$; that maximum is $a_i^Tz+\|a_i\|_2r$. Row norms are fixed coefficients. The first two inequalities give $z_1,z_2\ge r$, so $(2+\sqrt2)r\le6$. Equality is attained by $z_1=z_2=r=6/(2+\sqrt2)$.

(b) If more than $m$ coordinates are strictly between their bounds, the corresponding columns of $A$ are dependent. A nonzero direction supported there has $Ad=0$. A sufficiently short positive or negative move remains inside all bounds and keeps $Ax$ unchanged, contradicting extremality. Since a feasible $Q$ is bounded, it has an optimal vertex for any linear objective. At a vertex every coordinate except at most $m$ equals $0$ or its integer upper bound $U_j$, and is therefore integer. This does not claim that every interior coordinate is fractional or that an integral optimum always exists.

(c) A finite sequence may have exponentially many terms. The deck's enumeration considers up to $\binom nm$ bases; simplex visits adjacent bases and can take exponentially many pivots despite polynomial per-pivot work. The chapter develops simplex phases and certificates; ellipsoid, interior-point, separation-oracle, semidefinite and broader convex approaches occur only in its outlook. A claim about current research status would require a separate source check.
