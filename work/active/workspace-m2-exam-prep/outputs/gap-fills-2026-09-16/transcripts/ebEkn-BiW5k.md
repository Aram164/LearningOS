[00:57] This is an illustration of Simpson's paradox , a statistical paradox where it's possible
[01:01] to draw two opposite conclusions from the same data depending on how you divide things
[01:05] up, and statistics alone cannot help us solve it – we have to go outside statistics and
[01:09] understand the causality involved in the situation at hand.
[01:13] For example, if we know that humans get the disease more seriously and are therefore more
[01:17] likely to be prescribed treatment, then it can make sense that fewer individuals that
[01:21] get treated survive, even if the treatment increases the chances of recovery, since the
[01:25] individuals that got treated were more likely to die in the first place.
[01:28] On the other hand, if we know that humans, regardless of how sick they are, are more
[01:32] likely to get treated than cats because no one wants to pay for kitty healthcare, then
[01:36] the fact that 4 out of 5 humans died while only 1 in 5 cats died suggests that, indeed,
[01:42] the treatment may be a bad choice.

[01:48] causally related to the experiment influence how you apply your treatments, and if you
[01:52] have an uncontrolled experiment, you have to be able to take those outside biases into
[01:56] account.
